# Introduction

This page reproduces Chapter 1 of the report "The Benefit of Investing in Electricity Grids: A comparative analysis to 2050 for Latin America and the Caribbean" (Quirós-Tortós, Salazar-Vargas, Victor-Gallardo, Aldana Millán and Alarcón, Inter-American Development Bank).

(intro-regional-context)=
## Regional context

Without an electricity grid there is no development, resilience or energy security. Electricity transmission infrastructure has become the axis that will determine the capacity of Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) to sustain its economic development, guarantee energy security and strengthen the resilience of the system (Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025). The grid performs four functions simultaneously: i) it brings generation closer to consumption; ii) it sustains the stability and reliability of operation; iii) it widens the set of resources the system can draw on; and iv) it allows failures to be shared between regions. Its economic effects follow from these: by enabling the dispatch of the lowest-cost generation and displacing production from expensive fuels, transmission makes the system cheaper, sustains continuity of service and strengthens security of supply. For this reason, transmission is no longer a function subordinate to generation and has become a central component of the regional energy architecture (Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025).

The regional electricity mix has evolved rapidly. After decades of expansion based on large hydropower plants (about two thirds of the historical mix) and thermal plants (about one third), today more than 60% of regional generation comes from renewable sources, with notable growth in solar and wind (IEA, 2024; IRENA, 2025; OLADE, 2024). Solar and wind capacity grew by about 108 GW in the last decade, at a year-on-year rate close to 28% (OLADE, 2024), and this trend is expected to continue given the increasing competitiveness of these technologies. However, the best solar and, above all, wind potential is usually located in areas far from the main load centres, which critically links the use of low-cost generation to the timely expansion of the transmission system.

This transformation of supply is joined by a new wave of demand. Several studies estimate that regional electricity demand could increase by between 50% and 90% by 2040, equivalent to annual growth of between 2.4% and 3.6% (IEA, 2023), driven by the electrification of end uses in transport and industry. It is estimated, for example, that data centres could account for about 5% of regional demand in 2035 and that electric transport could reach about 3% of total demand by 2030 (OLADE, 2024; Michelena et al., 2023). This combination of greater penetration of variable renewables and sustained demand growth places increasing demands on grids that, in many countries, already operate at high levels of saturation.

The most visible consequence of saturation is the curtailment of renewable energy. Owing to grid constraints, inflexible contracts, reservoir management or insufficient demand, curtailment in the region reached 53,000 GWh in 2024 (approximately 3.2% of total regional generation), a figure equivalent to several times the annual electricity consumption of several countries in the region, and economic losses of about USD 7 billion per year (OLADE, 2025). The 2025 national figures confirm the upward trend. In Chile, renewable curtailment reached about 6,100 GWh in 2025, 7.8% more than in 2024 and about 7% of the country's total generation, while electricity demand grew by only 0.4% (CEN-Chile, 2026; ACERA, 2026). In Brazil, curtailment of centralised wind and solar generation climbed to about 20.6%, a historical maximum, partly because of grid constraints and partly because of distributed generation that reached about 44,000 GWh (ONS, 2025).

When transmission capacity is insufficient, part of this electricity must be curtailed and the operator must redispatch the system with generation forced by grid constraints; that is, units located on the congested side of the grid, which, depending on the location of the constraint, may be peaking plants or other available units. In critical situations, it may be necessary to curtail demand. Because in several systems of the region part of the fuel for that generation is imported, the higher system cost comes with greater dependence on fuel imports and with exposure to the volatility of international prices.

For all these reasons, the saturation of transmission capacity is today one of the main bottlenecks that keep the region dependent on imported fossil fuels. If left unaddressed, these challenges could translate into higher electricity system costs, lower reliability and growing exposure to disruptive events (WEF & IADB, 2025; IEA, 2020). To maintain their strategic role, the transmission grids of LAC must expand and modernise at a pace consistent with the speed at which supply and demand are being transformed, and thus sustain the secure operation of an increasingly complex and variable system.

The transmission system also plays an essential role in the technical and operational resilience of the system. Interconnecting regions with different climate exposure, resource availability and load profiles makes it possible to manage contingencies, share reserves and reduce vulnerability to extreme events or critical failures. In a context of increasing extreme weather events and new threats (such as cyberattacks), a secure, resilient and well-interconnected grid protects the continuity of supply and limits the economic and social costs of interruptions, which grow as the electrification of the economy advances. During the 2023–2024 El Niño episode, the hydropower share of generation in Colombia fell from 87% to 47% and the electricity price rose by about 200%, while in Ecuador low reservoir levels, combined with poor maintenance of thermal plants, forced rotating outages of up to 14 hours a day, electricity imports from Colombia and the leasing of floating thermal generation (Gil et al., in preparation). With a new El Niño episode projected for 2026–2027, with a probability of development of between 96% and 98% according to the projections compiled by ECLAC (NOAA CPC, 2026), the grid's capacity to move energy between areas with different climate exposure acquires immediate value.

Regional integration amplifies these benefits. Cross-border exchanges can reduce costs and lower dependence on fuel imports when planning, regulation and operation are coordinated between countries (IEA, 2024; Levy et al., 2020; OLADE, 2024). Their value multiplies in scenarios of greater hydrological variability, where interconnections become a key component not only of economic efficiency but also of reducing dependence on fuel imports and of system resilience. Fully exploiting this potential requires coordinated planning, regulatory harmonisation and investment in both national grids and binational and regional interconnections. Without a secure and resilient backbone grid in each country, regional integration is compromised: interconnections cannot be used if the internal grid cannot carry that energy to the consumption centres. ECLAC's prospective exercises reinforce this conclusion. In an analysis of scenarios for the electricity sector of Latin America to 2050, the high renewable penetration scenario with new interconnections (RE+I) reduces cumulative costs by about USD 124 billion compared with the same scenario without new interconnections (USD 119 billion in South America and USD 5 billion in Central America and Mexico), avoids 27.2 million tonnes of CO2 and requires 75 GW less installed capacity and 31.6 GW less battery capacity (Sabbatella et al., 2026). The same study finds that the integration potential is far from being exploited: in 2023, electricity trade represented only 3.2% of generation in South America and 4.1% in SIEPAC, compared with 10.7% in the European Union.

Taken together, the lag in transmission is already raising the costs and risks of the system. Quantifying how much that underinvestment costs, and the benefit of investing in time, is what this note seeks to assess.

(intro-investment-gap)=
## Transmission investment gap

The magnitude of the investment required to close the transmission gap is considerable and grows with the horizon of analysis. The most recent estimates point to a need of between USD 6 and 8 billion per year to 2030 to expand and modernise LAC grids in a stated-policies scenario, a figure that could triple by 2050 in an accelerated-action scenario (IEA, 2023; IEA, 2025). This investment must cover not only new lines but also reinforcements, repowering, digitalisation, automation and technologies that improve the resilience of the electricity system.

Against these needs, the observed flows are markedly insufficient. In 2022, only about USD 3.3 billion went to transmission works in the region (IEA, 2023; IEA, 2023b), a fraction of what is required, which reveals a critical investment and financing gap. Besides the shortage of capital, the gap reflects investors' perception of risk, the absence of clear signals from planning and regulation, and barriers in permitting and socio-environmental management. Environmental permits, prior consultation with communities and the establishment of rights of way add between two and four years to the schedule (Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025), so works are delayed by years even where capital is available and planning is settled. In addition, transmission still depends largely on public investment in a context of restricted fiscal space, even though it is estimated that about two thirds of the investment required in grids will have to come from private capital (IEA, 2023; Alvarez Pagliuca et al., 2022).

A distinctive feature of transmission is the mismatch between the lead times of generation and of grids. While renewable generation can be deployed in months, transmission lines require years between planning, permitting and construction, to which the lead times of the equipment supply chain are added. In LAC, transformers are the most critical equipment component, with manufacturing and transport lead times of close to two years (IEA, 2025).

International evidence confirms that the gap is global and that its consequences accumulate quickly. The IEA warns that grids have become the main bottleneck for integrating low-cost generation and for reducing dependence on fuel imports: there are more than 3,000 GW of wind and solar projects waiting for connection, five times the capacity installed in 2022, and about 1,650 GW of capacity at an advanced stage remain stalled because of insufficient grids (IEA, 2023). In its delay scenario, the IEA estimates that by 2030 about 80% of new global renewable capacity, more than 1,500 GW, could not be connected in time if grid expansion is not accelerated, with additional costs above USD 2 trillion by mid-century (IEA, 2023). The region is no stranger to this phenomenon. In Brazil, between March and May 2026 ANEEL revoked, at the request of the developers themselves, authorisations for solar projects totalling about 6.4 GW. In the most recent batch, of 3.6 GW, the most frequent justification was the lack of transmission margin to connect the plants, even though the regulator's tracking system records about 79 GW of solar projects and 14.7 GW of wind projects at different stages of development (pv magazine Brasil, 2026).

The challenge is also worldwide. To limit warming to 1.5 °C, IRENA estimates that annual spending on grids should double, from about USD 0.5 trillion in 2025 to about USD 1 trillion between 2026 and 2035, and rise to USD 1.2 trillion between 2036 and 2050 (IRENA, 2026). Although these are global figures, they confirm that grids must grow at the same pace as electricity demand and renewables.

Closing the gap requires not only mobilising larger volumes of investment but also aligning planning, regulation and financing from the early stages, so that the structural needs identified in the plans translate into executable and bankable projects (Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025).

(intro-systemic-risks)=
## Systemic risks of underinvestment

The study defines the "cost of not investing" as the additional cost borne by the electricity system when the grid expands only at the pace of its historical trend, instead of the pace the system itself would require, accumulated over the horizon of analysis. When grid capacity is insufficient, low-cost renewable energy cannot be evacuated, and the system dispatches more expensive generation.

This cost breaks down into direct and indirect components. It should also be noted that indirect effects and risks, such as the loss of social welfare, system resilience, lower security of supply, greater exposure to fossil fuels and fiscal effects, are not part of the cost quantification. This greater dependence on fossil fuels refers to the structural exposure of the system (to price volatility and to the availability of supply in emergencies), which is distinct from the higher direct fuel cost already accounted for in the more expensive generation. Quantifying these components makes it possible to express the cost of not investing in economic and technical terms, and to compare it directly with the cost of investing in time.

Finally, theory and evidence agree that the cost of not investing can far exceed the cost of the required investment. Therefore, in contexts of high uncertainty about the evolution of demand and the location of new generation, it is preferable to anticipate through additional capacity margins in the grid, even if their full use is delayed, rather than to underinvest and face much higher costs in the future (IEA, 2023; Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025). Transmission, because of its enabling and long-term nature, must be planned under robustness and resilience approaches that guarantee the lowest dependence on fuel imports and system efficiency.

(intro-international-evidence)=
## Comparative international evidence

The international literature consistently documents the benefits of expanding and modernising transmission and, by contrast, the costs of not doing so. The IEA identifies grid expansion as a central condition for integrating renewables and sustaining energy security (IEA, 2023). The evidence on transmission planning in Chile shows that regulatory and grid-infrastructure lags limit the integration of renewables and make the path to carbon neutrality more expensive, which calls for systemic action on the grid (Moreno et al., 2019). In the United States, analyses of advanced reconductoring and grid-enhancing technologies show that existing corridors can increase their effective capacity and accelerate the required expansion (O'Boyle et al., 2024; U.S. DOE, 2022). OECD studies estimate that a 2.5 °C warming scenario could imply losses of up to 5% of LAC GDP by 2050, part of which could be mitigated with more secure and resilient electricity systems (OECD et al., 2022).

The urgency of expanding and modernising grids has risen on the international agenda. At COP30 (Belém, 2025), the IDB Group presented the Power Transmission Acceleration Platform (PTAP), a regional initiative to support LAC countries in expanding and modernising their electricity grids. The platform was presented together with the Belém Declaration on Grids, endorsed by the RELAC member countries, which commits to accelerating investment in transmission and storage (IDB, 2025).

Regional evidence shows that these risks are already materialising. The curtailment documented in {ref}`intro-regional-context` is their most visible manifestation. The prospective regional analysis of transmission needs in the RELAC countries (Quirós-Tortós et al., 2025) sizes the grid expansion needs of the member countries. The same exercise shows that, without reconductoring, the need for new unplanned lines grows sharply and the system resorts to more expensive generation that is more intensive in fossil fuels (Quirós-Tortós et al., 2025). That same analysis identifies bottlenecks associated with the evacuation of renewables, the expansion of priority corridors and the coordination between generation and grid. In Chile, the coordination of grid expansion to integrate renewables and reduce the socio-environmental externalities of transmission has been documented in detail (Moreno et al., 2019).

Experience outside the region offers lessons applicable to each LAC subregion. The IEA documents that connection queues, long grid development lead times and the need to modernise existing assets are common challenges in systems with high penetration of renewable energy (IEA, 2023). Taken together, this international evidence makes it possible to size the magnitude of the problem and to draw good practices for accelerating transmission expansion in the region.

(intro-objective-scope)=
## Objective and scope of the study

This study addresses a central question: what is the cost of maintaining trend trajectories of transmission investment compared with an adequate investment path? Unlike analyses focused exclusively on investment projections or on institutional barriers, this technical note seeks to quantify explicitly the cost of not investing, translating it into comparable and communicable metrics for decision-makers.

The note is conceived as a quantitative complement to the report "Unlocking the grid: how to guarantee reliable and sustainable energy in Latin America and the Caribbean" (Alarcón and Quirós-Tortós, 2025), which diagnosed the structural, regulatory and technological barriers that hold back the expansion of electricity transmission. While the earlier report proposes a public-policy roadmap around five pillars (planning, regulation, financing, permitting and implementation, and technological innovation), the present study provides quantitative evidence of the value of acting in time, by estimating the difference in system costs between scenarios with an optimisation model that represents the electricity system (generation, transmission, storage, demand and interconnections) of the countries analysed.

The geographical scope covers 19 LAC countries: Argentina, Barbados, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Haiti, Honduras, Mexico, Nicaragua, Panama, Paraguay, Peru, the Dominican Republic and Uruguay. Together, these countries account for about 90% of the installed capacity and electricity generation of the region. They include its three largest systems, Brazil, Mexico and Argentina, and all the continental transmission grids. The main systems not included in the study are Venezuela and Cuba, owing to lack of data when the regional model was developed.

The time horizon extends to 2050, consistent with the typical construction lead times of transmission infrastructure in the region, which range between 8 and 12 years once the pre-feasibility, environmental permitting, rights-of-way, financing and commissioning stages are considered. This horizon captures both the short-term effects of underinvestment and its cumulative long-term consequences.

The results are aimed at national governments, including sector ministries, at regional electricity-sector planners, at regulators of the regional market and at decision-makers of the IDB and other regional and global entities. The purpose is to offer a solid quantitative basis for assessing investment in transmission as a structural commitment to security of supply, system resilience and regional economic competitiveness.

The scope of that quantification should be made clear. The central indicator of the study is the direct cost of the electricity system: investment in generation, transmission and storage, operation and maintenance, and fuel. Left out of that accounting are the benefits that a stronger grid also delivers and that the model does not value by design: reliability and quality of service, operational flexibility in the face of renewable variability, and resilience to extreme weather events or other contingencies. Nor are the environmental and health externalities associated with fossil generation internalised, nor the fiscal and macroeconomic effects of a higher fuel import bill, nor the savings from lower GHG emissions, sales of carbon credits and other derived environmental benefits. All these effects operate in the same direction as the reported results, so the figures in this study should be read as a conservative estimate of the benefit of investing in time.
